The tide tables were wrong by eleven minutes

By Amara Okafor · 14 March 2026

The tide tables for the estuary were first printed in 1867, on a hand press that still stands in the harbour office. For a century and a half they were corrected by hand each winter, from readings taken at the old gauge under the lifeboat slip.

Last spring the gauge was replaced by a pressure sensor that reports every six minutes. The harbour board expected the new readings to confirm the printed tables. They did not: high water now arrives eleven minutes earlier than the tables say, and stands four centimetres higher.

Dr. Amara Okafor, who leads the survey, says the difference has been building for decades. Dredging of the main channel in the 1970s let the tide run in faster, and the sea itself has risen. The hand corrections absorbed the change a few minutes at a time, so nobody saw it whole.

For the pilots who bring coasters over the bar, eleven minutes matters. A loaded vessel has about forty minutes of safe water on a neap tide. The board will publish new tables in January and keep the sensor data open, so that anyone can check the predictions against what the water actually did.
